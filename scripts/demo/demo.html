A field guide to scrolling

Everything you never wanted to know about moving a page up and down

Most of what we read on a computer is taller than the screen it sits on. Documents, articles, code and conversations all spill off the bottom edge, and getting to the rest of them is something we do thousands of times a day without thinking about it.

There are many ways to do it: dragging a scroll bar, pressing Page Down, spinning a wheel, sliding two fingers across a touchpad. Each one trades precision for speed in its own way.

The scroll bar

The oldest tool is the scroll bar. Its thumb shows how much of the page you can see and where you are in it, and dragging the thumb jumps straight to any part of a long document.

It is precise, but it asks you to move the pointer away from what you are reading, to the very edge of the window, and back again.

The keyboard

Arrow keys move a line at a time, Page Up and Page Down move a screen at a time, and Home and End jump to either end. It is fast once your hands are already on the keys, and useless when they are not.

The wheel

The scroll wheel put scrolling under one finger. Every notch moves the page a few lines, which is perfect for reading and tiring for long distances: getting through a long page means spinning the wheel over and over again.

Spin, spin, spin, lift your finger, start again.

Free-spinning wheels and touchpads help, but they make it hard to hold a slow, steady pace while you read.

Autoscroll

Autoscroll turns the middle button into a throttle. Click it, and a small marker appears where you clicked. Move the mouse a little below the marker and the page starts to drift down, slowly enough to read along. Move further and it speeds up. Move above the marker and it scrolls back up.

The pointer turns into an arrow that shows which way the page is moving. When you are done, click again, or press any key, and it stops right where you are.

Because the speed depends on the distance from the marker, you can settle into a comfortable reading pace and leave your hand still. Long log files, documentation and threads become something you can simply read from top to bottom.

If you prefer, hold the middle button down instead of clicking it: the page scrolls while you hold it and stops the moment you let go.

Where it came from

Autoscroll arrived with the first wheel mice, where pressing the wheel down was the way to scroll faster than spinning it. It has been part of Windows web browsers and office apps ever since, and people who learned it there tend to miss it everywhere else.

Everywhere

On GNOME it can now work in every app, not just a browser: editors, file managers, chat apps, settings, music players and terminals that want it. Middle-click on a link still opens it in a new tab, and middle-click on a tab still closes it.

And when you need the middle button for something else, hold Ctrl and it behaves exactly as it always has.
